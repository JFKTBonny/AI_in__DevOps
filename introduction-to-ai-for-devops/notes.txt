INTRODUCTION TO AI FOR DEVOPS
=============================

Purpose
-------
These notes explain how traditional AI, generative AI, and large language
models (LLMs) can be used in DevOps. The examples are illustrative: an AI
system can support an engineer's judgment, but it should not be treated as
automatically correct or as a replacement for operational safeguards.


1. What does AI mean in DevOps?
-------------------------------
DevOps teams operate software and infrastructure while trying to deliver
changes reliably. Their work produces and depends on many kinds of
information: metrics, logs, traces, configuration files, deployment history,
runbooks, incident reports, and alerts.

AI describes a broad set of computational approaches that perform tasks such
as finding patterns, classifying information, making predictions, or
generating content. In DevOps, these methods can help people make sense of
operational data, reduce repetitive work, and investigate incidents. The
useful method depends on the task and the quality of the available data.

Examples:
- Predict whether a service is likely to breach a latency objective.
- Group similar alerts so that responders can focus on one underlying issue.
- Summarize a large set of logs during an incident.
- Draft a deployment manifest from a clearly specified requirement.

AI output is a recommendation or prediction, not proof. Engineers should
validate it against system state, test results, and operational policy.


2. Traditional and predictive AI
---------------------------------
In these notes, "traditional AI" refers mainly to machine-learning and
rule-based methods that classify, score, detect, or predict. It is a broad
informal label, not one specific algorithm.

Some systems use explicit rules, such as "alert when CPU usage is above 90%
for five minutes." Other systems learn patterns from historical examples or
from a baseline of normal behavior. Machine-learning methods can work with
structured data such as time-series metrics, but they can also process text,
images, and other data when designed for those inputs. They do not all require
labeled data.

Common DevOps applications:
- Classification: label an event as likely to be an error, warning, or
  routine event.
- Forecasting: estimate future resource use from measurements over time.
- Anomaly detection: flag observations that differ from an expected pattern.
- Clustering: group similar incidents, alerts, or log messages.

Example: detecting a possible service incident
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Suppose a service normally has low, steady request latency. A monitoring
system observes that latency has risen sharply while error counts and CPU
usage have also increased. A rule-based alert could fire when a threshold is
crossed. A predictive model could estimate the likelihood of an impending
service-level objective breach from recent measurements.

The alert is a signal to investigate, not a diagnosis. A responder might
check recent deployments, traffic changes, dependency health, and resource
pressure before deciding what action to take.

Strengths:
- Can continuously process large volumes of measurements.
- Can give consistent results for well-defined tasks.
- Can identify trends or unusual behavior that are hard to spot manually.

Limitations:
- Thresholds and learned patterns can produce false alarms or miss real
  problems.
- A model may perform poorly when the service, workload, or data changes.
- Historical data may reflect past incidents or operational bias.
- A prediction usually does not explain the full cause of an incident.
- Poorly chosen metrics or incomplete data lead to unreliable results.


3. Generative AI
----------------
Generative AI creates new content in response to input. A text-based
generative model can draft or transform natural language, code, configuration,
and summaries. Large language models are one kind of generative AI.

In a DevOps workflow, a model might:
- Summarize logs or an incident timeline supplied in a prompt.
- Explain a configuration file or command.
- Draft a runbook, shell script, Dockerfile, or infrastructure manifest.
- Turn an incident discussion into a concise status update.

Example: investigating a Kubernetes pod
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
An engineer supplies relevant pod events, container logs, resource limits,
and recent change details. A generative model may summarize likely causes,
such as a failed health check, an unavailable image, or memory exhaustion, and
suggest checks to distinguish among them.

The quality of the result depends on the information supplied. A model cannot
inspect a cluster unless it has been connected to tools that provide that
access. Even with tool access, it may miss important context or suggest an
incorrect fix. Check its claims against Kubernetes events, metrics, logs, and
the actual configuration.

Strengths:
- Can work with natural-language questions and unstructured text.
- Can produce readable explanations and adapt the response to a given
  context.
- Can help create a first draft quickly.

Limitations:
- It may produce plausible-sounding but false statements, commands, or
  configuration (often called hallucinations).
- It may misunderstand incomplete or ambiguous instructions.
- Its answer may omit important operational constraints.
- Generated code can contain bugs, insecure settings, or destructive
  commands.
- Sending logs, configuration, or incident data to an external service may
  expose sensitive information; follow organizational data policies.

Use generated commands and configuration only after review and testing.
Prefer read-only investigation before actions that change production systems.
Require explicit human approval and appropriate access controls for
high-impact changes.


4. Traditional AI compared with generative AI
---------------------------------------------
These categories can overlap: a product may use prediction, rules, and
generative models together. The table describes common differences, not
absolute rules.

Dimension              Predictive / rule-based AI       Generative AI
---------------------  --------------------------------  -------------------------------
Typical goal           Classify, score, detect, predict  Create or transform content
Typical input          Metrics, events, labeled examples Logs, documents, prompts, code
Typical output         Score, class, alert, forecast     Text, code, summary, suggestion
Example                Flag unusual error rate           Explain an incident timeline
Main concern           Drift, false alerts, data quality Incorrect or unsafe generated output

Both approaches depend on relevant input, careful evaluation, and monitoring.
Neither should be assumed accurate simply because it uses AI.


5. Large language models (LLMs)
-------------------------------
An LLM is a model trained on large amounts of text to process and generate
language. Many current LLMs use transformer neural-network architectures.
During generation, a model uses the prompt and its learned parameters to
produce likely next tokens (small units of text), one step at a time.

An LLM can be useful for explaining, summarizing, drafting, and answering
questions about supplied context. It does not inherently know the current
state of a system, verify that a command works, or guarantee that an answer
is true. Its knowledge may be incomplete or out of date. Some systems can
retrieve documents or call tools; those capabilities come from the surrounding
application and integrations, not from language generation alone.

Common terms:
- Prompt: the instructions and context provided to a model.
- Token: a chunk of text processed by the model; it may be a word, part of a
  word, or punctuation.
- Context window: the amount of input and conversation the model can consider
  at one time.
- Hallucination: generated content that is stated confidently but is
  unsupported or incorrect.


6. Responsible use in DevOps
----------------------------
- Give the model relevant, sanitized context rather than secrets or unrelated
  data.
- Ask for assumptions and evidence, and verify important claims independently.
- Test generated scripts and configuration in a safe environment.
- Review permissions, networking, resource limits, and security implications.
- Use version control and normal review procedures for generated changes.
- Keep humans in the approval path for production-impacting actions.
- Measure outcomes, such as alert precision, time to diagnosis, and rollback
  rate, rather than relying on a demonstration alone.


Key takeaway
------------
Predictive and rule-based AI are often useful for detecting patterns and
estimating what may happen. Generative AI and LLMs are often useful for
working with language and drafting explanations or artifacts. They can be
combined, but both require good context, validation, and operational
guardrails.
